# Hazards, Conditions & Flavour

*Text pools for the info panel. The job of this text is to make a generated
body feel like a **place** — somewhere with a specific character you could
write a scene in.*

---

## Tone

Plain, concrete, slightly wry. A competent survey officer writing notes for
someone who has to actually go there.

**Do:** name the specific danger · give a relatable comparison · say what would
happen to a person · leave hooks unexplained

**Don't:** use jargon without explanation · be melodramatic · use Kelvin, solar
masses, or unexplained units · describe local settlements (that's the user's
job, not the generator's)

> **From v2:** the quoted "settler flavour" lines were removed. They repeated
> too often and described local details at a planetary scale. Text should
> describe the *body*, not invent inhabitants.

---

## Hazard severity

Every body gets a hazard rating. Not everything is lethal — **mundane is
allowed and welcome**. A merely inconvenient world is a fine result and makes
the dangerous ones land harder.

| Rating | Meaning | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Benign** | You could stand here in ordinary clothing | rare, and delightful when it happens |
| **Mild** | Uncomfortable. Suit recommended, survivable without | common |
| **Hazardous** | Suit required. Manageable with equipment | common |
| **Severe** | Specialist equipment. Time-limited exposure | uncommon |
| **Lethal** | Kills quickly. Remote operations only | uncommon |
| **Absolute** | Nothing survives approach | rare — compact objects mostly |

Rating derives from computed temperature, pressure, gravity, atmosphere and
traits — never rolled independently of the picture.

## "What would kill you first"

The single most useful line for a GM. One sentence naming the *specific*
first-order danger.

### Temperature
- The cold. Unprotected exposure kills in under a minute.
- The heat. Surface temperature is above the melting point of lead.
- Nothing dramatic — you'd simply get very cold, very slowly.
- Thermal shock at the terminator. Stepping from day to night is a 300-degree change.
- The day side. Your radiators will fail before your suit does.
- The night side. The air itself is freezing out onto the ground.
- Chasing the twilight. Stand still long enough and the climate kills you.

### Atmosphere
- Nothing to breathe. You have as long as you can hold your breath.
- The air is breathable but the dust isn't. Filters clog in hours.
- Pressure. At sea level it's like being 400 metres underwater.
- There's no atmosphere at all — your fluids would boil.
- Trace hydrogen sulphide. You'd smell it once, then stop smelling anything.

### Pressure & depth
- Depth. Descend far enough and the hull simply stops being a hull.
- The pressure gradient. Ascending too fast is as dangerous as descending too far.
- Nothing to land on. You either stay flying or you keep falling.

### Radiation
- Radiation. The magnetosphere focuses it rather than deflecting it.
- Stellar flares, without warning and without a safe interval.
- The accretion disc. It's brighter than a galaxy and it doesn't care.

### Weather & terrain
- The wind. It carries enough grit to strip paint in minutes.
- It rains glass, sideways, at the speed of a bullet.
- Lightning. Continuous, planet-wide, and drawn to anything metallic.
- Ammonia rain. Everything organic dissolves; most seals don't last either.
- Cryovolcanic vents that erupt without seismic warning.

### Gravity & motion
- Gravity. At twice Earth normal, a fall is usually a fatal one.
- Almost no gravity at all. A careless push and you're in orbit.
- Tidal forces. They'd pull you apart lengthwise before you arrived.

### Nothing much
- Honestly, very little. Bring a coat.
- Nothing in particular. It's a quiet, unremarkable place.
- Boredom, mostly. There is very little here.

## Notable conditions

Weirder environmental facts. Roughly 1 in 3 bodies gets one; they're the
memorable detail.

### Precipitation
- It rains diamonds in the deep atmosphere — carbon compressed out of solution.
- It rains molten glass, driven sideways by supersonic winds.
- Snow falls here, but it's carbon dioxide.
- Ammonia rain falls for centuries in the same region without pause.
- Metallic rain — iron condenses in the upper atmosphere and falls as droplets.
- It rains, evaporates before landing, and rains again. Nothing reaches the ground.

### Light & sky
- The sky is the wrong colour and nobody agrees what to call it.
- Two sunrises per day, at right angles.
- Permanent twilight — the sun never rises or sets.
- The sun hasn't moved in the sky since the world formed.
- One hemisphere has never seen daylight. The ice there is original.
- The aurora is visible from the surface at all latitudes, constantly.
- It's bright enough at midnight to read by, from ring-light alone.
- The nearest star is close enough to show a visible disc, not a point.

### Motion & time
- A day here lasts longer than a year.
- The planet rotates backwards relative to its orbit.
- The whole body pulses on a cycle you can feel in your chest.
- It's spinning fast enough to be visibly flattened at the poles.
- Time runs measurably slower here. Not enough to notice; enough to measure.

### Geology & structure
- The crust rings like a struck bell after any significant impact.
- There are caverns beneath the crust larger than most moons.
- The core is offset from the geometric centre. Nobody has explained why.
- Half the crust is missing. The exposed mantle has cooled into a glass plain.
- The magnetic field flips on a decade-long cycle rather than a geological one.

### Sensory
- Sensors are useless here. So is long-range communication.
- The interference has a pattern. Probably natural.
- Ice at the poles is old enough to hold a readable atmospheric record.
- Something down there is warmer than it has any right to be.

## Resource notes

What makes a body worth visiting commercially — a resource-extraction hook.

- Heavy metals near enough the surface to be worth the trip.
- Water ice in quantity, and shallow.
- Rare-earth deposits concentrated by ancient volcanic activity.
- Helium-3 in the upper atmosphere. Easy scooping, hard shipping.
- Deuterium-rich oceans.
- Crystalline formations that regrow after harvest on a decadal cycle.
- Nothing of obvious value, which is its own kind of useful.
- Stable ground and a workable atmosphere — the rarest resources of all.
- Exotic isotopes in the mantle that shouldn't form naturally.

## Approach notes

Practical arrival advice. Useful for framing a scene.

- Straightforward approach. Plenty of stable ground for landing.
- Aerobraking is viable, if you trust your heat shielding.
- No landing sites. Everything is either liquid, molten, or moving.
- Approach on the night side. The day side will cook your radiators.
- The ring system makes a clean approach vector hard to find.
- Debris in orbit. Not all of it is natural.
- Do not approach. Observation from distance only.
- The gravity well is deep. Getting down is easy; getting back up is expensive.

## Per-family templates

Each archetype uses its own stat template — a black hole's card shares almost
nothing with a moon's.

### Stats derive from parameters, not from a separate roll

**The rule: numbers must never contradict the picture.** Every stat line below
that could be invented independently must instead be *read off* the same
parameters that drew the image. If the crust is visibly molten, the temperature
line cannot say −40 °C.

| Parameter | Stat lines it drives |
|---|---|
| **Ocean depth** | "Surface" (what's underfoot), water/ice mentions, humidity conditions |
| **Interior heat** | Surface temp, volcanic hazards, **magnetic field → radiation hazard** (a dead world has no dynamo, so its radiation rating must be worse) |
| **Zones** (tidal lock etc.) | Temperature becomes a **range across zones**, not one figure — "Dayside 430 °C · Nightside −170 °C" |
| **Cohesion** (asteroid) | Structural stability, whether landing or docking is safe |
| **Luminosity source** (nebula) | Visibility and sensor range |
| **Stellar activity** | Flare behaviour, approach note, radiation danger |
| **Field strength** (compact) | Radiation hazard, "how far away is safe" |
| **Accretion rate** (black hole) | Disc temperature, brightness, whether the "biggest danger" is the disc or the hole |
| **Operational status** (machine world) | Population, Status, whether power output is nonzero |
| **Hull integrity** (machine world) | The Hull integrity line directly, plus docking availability |

Two consequences worth stating outright:

- **A zoned body has no single surface temperature.** Its template must accept a
  range or a per-zone breakdown. This is the strongest layperson framing the
  tool produces — "a habitable ribbon between them" beats any single number.
- **Interior heat at 0 means no magnetic field.** That should show up as a
  harsher radiation hazard, which is a satisfying chain: a visual choice about
  the core produces a survival fact on the card.

### Where the temperature numbers come from

**`details.climate`, and nowhere else.** The generator exposes a per-face
thermal summary derived from the same field the frosting colours itself from,
so a card and the picture cannot disagree — the standing rule above, made
mechanical.

**It is present on EVERY body.** It used to be `null` unless the Tidal locking
dial was up, because the thermal field only existed there — which meant an
ordinary rotating planet, i.e. most of them, had no temperature a card could
quote. `CC.Climate` now builds a field on every body from Starlight, Interior
heat and latitude, folding the tidal-lock zone in as one contributor when the
dial is up. Use `spread` to decide whether to quote a range or a single figure:
a locked or strongly tilted world has a large one, an ordinary world a small
one.

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `base` | the body's own climate, 0..1, before any zoning |
| `min` / `max` / `mean` | temperature across the circumference, 0..1 |
| `spread` | `max - min` — **this is "thermal shock at the terminator"** |
| `states` | fraction of the circumference in each surface state |
| `hottest` / `coldest` | the state at the hottest and coldest bearings |
| `hottestAt` / `coldestAt` | which bearing those are, in degrees |
| `frozenFraction` | how much of the circumference is cold or frozen — the one-line answer to "does this world have ice" |
| `radiation` | 0..1 radiation hazard, from Star activity and how much atmosphere shields the ground |
| `activity` / `shield` | the two terms `radiation` is built from |

Temperatures are **normalized 0..1, not degrees.** Turning that into a figure is
the archetype's business: a star's 0.9 is not a planet's 0.9. The mapping lives
with the per-family template, not here.

### Surface states

`climate.surfaceStateAt(angle)` returns one of five bands. They are wide on
purpose — the useful reading is which band a face is in, and a hairline between
two would make a label flicker between neighbouring bearings.

| State | Reads as | Volatile behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| `boiled` | scorched, bare, ashen | any sea has boiled off; ground is burnt |
| `hot` | arid, sparse cover | liquid unstable at the surface |
| `temperate` | ordinary ground cover | liquid stable — the habitable band |
| `cold` | frost, thinning cover | liquid freezing at the margins |
| `frozen` | ice cap, sea ice | volatiles frozen out; the atmosphere thins here |

**An ordinary world is a sentence about latitude.** "Temperate at the equator,
frozen at the poles" is now available on every body, not only on locked ones —
and on a strongly tilted world it inverts, which is worth naming when it does.

**A violent star is a separate fact from a hot one.** `radiation` is driven by
Star activity and by how much atmosphere stands in the way, never by
temperature, so "cold, and lethally irradiated" is a reachable and useful
combination. An airless world under an active star is the worst case, which is
the satisfying chain the section above asks for: a visual choice about the
atmosphere produces a survival fact on the card.

**A locked world is a sentence about two of these at once.** "Boiled on one
face, frozen on the other, with a habitable ribbon between them" is the single
most useful line the tool can produce, and every part of it is read off the
picture rather than invented beside it.

Text that names a face should say which — "the day side", "the night side",
"the terminator" — because on a locked world *where* is half the fact.

| < −200 | Cold enough to freeze air solid |

## Naming

Generated names combine:

- **Stem** — invented syllables (`Ka` + `ross` + `a`)
- **Greek letter** — ~25% of the time (`Kappa Verossa`) — real catalogue convention
- **Catalogue tag** — ~8% (`Verossa KX-2291`)
- **Suffix** — sometimes (`Prime`, `Minor`, `Deep`, `III`, `Reach`)

Never combine Greek letter *and* catalogue tag — the result reads as noise.

Name style is user-selectable; see [PARAMETERS.md](PARAMETERS.md).

## Extending these lists

They should grow. Guidelines:

- **Specific beats generic.** "It rains glass sideways" beats "hostile weather".
- **Concrete consequences.** Say what happens to a person.
- **Leave hooks unexplained.** "Nobody has explained why" invites a story.
- **Keep some mundane.** Not everything is extreme. Contrast is what makes the
  extremes land.
- **No inhabitants.** Describe the place; let the user populate it.
